## best-possible-history
title: The best possible medication history
keywords: medication reconciliation, home medication, admission, history, omission, discrepancy, provincial drug history, dhdr
Admission reconciliation compares a verified list of what the patient actually takes at home with the admission orders. Each difference is either intentional (and should be documented) or unintentional (and should be corrected). Omissions are the most common unintentional discrepancy.

## intentional-discrepancy
title: Intentional and unintentional discrepancies
keywords: omission, dose change, frequency, held, stopped, intentional, unintentional, document, reason
A medication may be held on purpose (nil by mouth, low blood pressure, bleeding, surgery). The reason belongs in the record so that the next team does not take the gap for an error and the drug is restarted at the right time.

## new-without-indication
title: New medications need an indication
keywords: indication, new medication, diagnosis, problem list, pantoprazole, omeprazole, proton pump inhibitor, quetiapine, antipsychotic, deprescribing
Every new medication should map to a diagnosis or a clear purpose. Acid suppression started in hospital without a continuing reason (gastric protection with an NSAID, steroid or dual antiplatelet therapy; bleeding; reflux) is a frequent example of a drug carried on without an indication; antipsychotics started for delirium are another.

## discharge-list
title: The discharge medication list
keywords: discharge, discharge prescription, new, changed, stopped, continued, home, patient instructions, summary
At discharge the list is sorted into new, changed, stopped and continued medicines against what the patient took before admission, so the patient, the family physician and the community pharmacist see the same plan. Hospital-only medicines (intravenous antibiotics, injections, as-needed doses) normally stop at discharge.

## duplicate-therapy
title: Therapeutic duplication
keywords: duplicate, therapeutic duplication, same class, two drugs, statin, proton pump inhibitor, opioid, antiplatelet, anticoagulant, ssri
Two drugs from the same class at once usually mean a substitution that was not completed (a home drug and its formulary equivalent) or a prescribing error. Deliberate combinations, such as dual antiplatelet therapy after a coronary stent, should be documented as intended.
