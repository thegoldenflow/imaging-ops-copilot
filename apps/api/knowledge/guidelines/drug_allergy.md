## allergy-check
title: Acting on an allergy alert
keywords: allergy, allergic, anaphylaxis, hives, rash, criticality, blocking, pharmacist, alternative
An order that matches a documented allergy by drug or class must not be given until a person has checked it. Look at what the reaction was (anaphylaxis or swelling versus a mild rash or a side effect recorded as an allergy) and whether the patient has taken the drug since. Choose an alternative, or document why the order stands.

## beta-lactam
title: Penicillin and cephalosporin allergy
keywords: penicillin, amoxicillin, piperacillin, cephalosporin, ceftriaxone, cefazolin, beta-lactam, cross-reactivity, allergy
Most reported penicillin allergies are not confirmed on testing. Cross-reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins is low, and mostly limited to drugs with similar side chains; a cephalosporin is often acceptable after a non-severe penicillin reaction, with monitoring. A severe or immediate reaction to a class means avoiding that class.

## nsaid-aspirin
title: Aspirin and NSAID hypersensitivity
keywords: aspirin, nsaid, ibuprofen, naproxen, ketorolac, celecoxib, asthma, urticaria, cross-reactivity, allergy
Patients who react to aspirin often react to other NSAIDs that inhibit COX-1, because the reaction is pharmacological rather than immune. Treat an aspirin allergy as an NSAID-class allergy; acetaminophen at usual doses is generally tolerated.

## opioid-sulfonamide
title: Opioid and sulfonamide allergy
keywords: morphine, hydromorphone, tramadol, opioid, sulfonamide, sulfamethoxazole, allergy, itching
Itching or flushing with morphine is often histamine release, not allergy; a true opioid allergy is rare and a different opioid class may be tolerated. Sulfonamide antibiotic allergy does not mean avoiding non-antibiotic sulfonamides, but sulfamethoxazole itself must be avoided.
